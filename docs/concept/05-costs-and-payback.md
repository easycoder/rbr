# Costs and payback

> **Audience:** industry — specifiers, heating contractors, developers, housing providers · **Status:** draft · **Last verified:** 2026-09-21 · **See also:** [Why room by room](01-why-room-by-room.md) · [Deployment and roles](04-deployment-and-roles.md) · [Glossary](../appendix/glossary.md)

## The cost side

A system costs what its parts cost, plus a trade's time to fit them, and it scales with the number of rooms and radiators rather than with the size of the house. The parts are ordinary items — a small computer, a thermometer for each room, a relay for each radiator, and the valves themselves — so the material cost can be quoted rather than guessed. There is nothing bespoke to inflate it, and nothing to buy again each year.

Whether it pays for itself, and how quickly, therefore comes down to a single unknown: the size of the saving. It is worth being straight about how well that is known.

## Where a saving comes from

Room-by-room control saves energy by not heating rooms nobody is using, and it does so without asking anyone to remember.

Under a conventional system the whole house answers to one thermostat, and the only way to keep an empty room out of it is for somebody to go round and turn that radiator down — and then to remember to turn it up again before the room is wanted. In a quiet household with a firm routine that happens often enough. In a busy one, with children, it usually does not: the morning goes on getting everyone out of the door, and the radiators are left as they were, heating rooms that will be empty until the afternoon.

There is a second and smaller effect on the plant: a house asked for heat room by room is asked for less, and asked more steadily, which suits a heat pump in particular. It is real, but it is second to the first.

And there is a third, which works on the household rather than on the plant. Cost, shown plainly and often, changes what people do: a driver who can watch the live fuel consumption tends to drive more gently, and somebody who reads the meter every day becomes less casual about leaving things on. A system that makes a household's own heating visible can work the same way, turning a bill that arrives once a quarter into something the household can see itself influencing. The record this needs already exists — RBR notes each room's demand and its achieved temperature — so showing the pattern is a presentational step rather than a new instrument.

Not every household responds to that, and the least likely are those for whom the cost is immaterial; but they are not the market. For most households the cost of living is a live concern, and a system that makes their own consumption visible is offering them something they can act on — the same instinct as eating sensibly or driving steadily, and most people are glad of the help.

## Why there is no figure to quote

Two reasons, and neither of them is a matter of effort.

The first is that there is no before-and-after. Neither of the two systems in service has a measured history from the period before it was installed, and two installations are too few to generalise from in any case.

The second is deeper, and it is why this document is about behaviour rather than engineering. The saving is not a property of the system; it is a property of the household. A household that already turns its radiators down and sets its programmer carefully has very little to gain. A household that does not has a great deal. And installing the system removes the very behaviour it would have been measured against, so the counterfactual — what this household would otherwise have spent — is no longer observable once the system is in.

## What can be said

What can be said is where the waste sits, and roughly how much of it there is. The commonest case is one or two rooms left heated through the day while nobody is in them: the child's bedroom after the school run, the spare room, the study. A rough estimate puts that at somewhere between a tenth and a third of the day, for those rooms, in households that do not manage their heating closely.

So the honest claim is directional. There is a real saving to be had, it is largest where heating is currently managed least, and its size will vary between households far more than it varies between systems. Anyone who quotes one percentage for every home is guessing.

Payback follows from two unknowns — what the installation costs, and how much of the household's waste it removes — so it is best stated as a condition rather than a promise. In a household at the diligent end of the range, a system is a comfort and convenience purchase. In one at the other end, it can pay for itself.

## How a figure would be established

A number worth publishing needs a trial rather than an anecdote: matched houses, one group with the system and one without, run through a heating season, with the households not selected for how keen they already are. RBR keeps its own record of each room's demand and achieved temperature, so a measurement is possible in principle — it is arithmetic that needs installations, not instrumentation.
