# Why room by room

> **Audience:** industry — specifiers, heating contractors, developers, housing providers · **Status:** draft · **Last verified:** 2026-09-20 · **See also:** [The experience is the product](00-the-experience-is-the-product.md) · [What RBR is](02-what-rbr-is.md) · [Glossary](../appendix/glossary.md)

## The house has changed; the way we heat it has not

A new house arrives with more than it used to. Solar panels on the roof. Increasingly, a heat pump where a gas boiler used to be. A broadband router fitted as routinely as the mains wiring. And, unchanged from a hundred years ago, a radiator in every room — all of them on one circuit, all of them answering to a single thermostat in a hallway or on a landing.

The building has been rebuilt around energy. The controls have barely moved.

That gap is the opportunity. The pieces needed to heat a house room by room already exist, are already in the house, and are already inexpensive. What has been missing is a way to fit them together that a heating engineer would be glad to install and that a landlord would be glad to support.

## Why room-by-room control is the next step

Two changes make it matter more than it used to.

**Heat spent on rooms nobody is using is now worth avoiding.** A house held at one temperature spends energy keeping spare bedrooms, hallways and empty rooms warm, because a single thermostat cannot tell the difference between them. A house that heats only where it is needed asks less of the system, and the same comfort costs less to deliver.

**A heat pump rewards being asked gently.** Heat pumps run most efficiently at low flow temperatures and long, steady run times. They work against themselves when they are pushed to satisfy the coldest room, or when they cycle to correct an overshoot they cannot see. A system that knows the temperature in every room changes what the plant is asked to do: demand follows occupancy rather than a single hallway average.

How large that effect is depends on the house and the plant. The point is directional and it is the same direction as every other efficiency measure in the building — ask for less, and ask for it steadily.

## Why it has not happened yet

It is not for want of parts. Mains-powered radiator valve actuators, wireless room thermometers and small controllers are all commodity items. Room-by-room control is rare because it has never been anyone's whole job. The heating is fitted by one trade, the controls specified by another, and the network left to nobody. A homeowner who wants it today buys a bundle of consumer gadgets, each with its own app and account, and none of them anybody's job to support.

There is also no settled national strategy joining the pieces up. Support for all-electric heating has moved in both directions and will go on doing so; that is a short-term argument, measured in parliaments. Over a ten-year horizon the direction is one-way. Homes will get their heat from electricity, electricity will not be cheap, and a heating system that does not waste it will not be a luxury. The gap is not conviction. It is a workable, standard way to fit the controls.

## The path cars took

Through the twentieth century, a heater and a radio moved from optional extras to standard fittings in a car. Not because every buyer asked for them one at a time, but because manufacturers found that including them won market share at a trivial marginal cost once the wiring and the dashboard were there anyway.

Solar panels, heat pumps and a router are following the same path into the fabric of a new house. Room-by-room control is the next item on that list. The reason to fit it early is the same as it was in the car: once it is standard, it is expected.

## Learning from the first wave

Room-by-room control is not a new idea. Through the early 2020s the major controls manufacturers brought systems to market that did much of what RBR does, backed by advertising budgets to match, and the take-up never followed the marketing: several years on, networked heating controls of any kind still sit in only a minority of homes.

The evidence points to friction at the point of installation. Surveys of installers consistently name products that are hard to install as the commonest cause of a delayed job, with compatibility problems close behind, and about a quarter of installers report difficulty even reaching manufacturer support. Government studies reach the same conclusion from the other side: that advanced heating controls are often too complex and non-intuitive, and that most homes do not have the full set of controls building regulations already call for.

What makes a room-by-room system hard is rarely the plumbing. It is the network. A conventional system asks for a hub, a bridge or a gateway, a phone app, an account, and a wireless connection for every device — another thing to pair, another to get onto the household Wi-Fi, another to fail when the router is replaced. And because the saving only arrives once every radiator has been fitted, the cost and the fiddling land together, well before the household sees any benefit.

The lesson is that demand was never the problem. Householders asked for room-by-room control in the same research in which installers named complexity as the barrier; the appetite was there, and the products stalled anyway. What was missing was a system that arrives ready to work, and someone the household can ask when it does not.

## What a workable solution has to do

For the trade to take it up, a solution has to clear two bars — and both of them are about the experience rather than the parts.

1. **A heating engineer can fit and commission it.** Parts bought off the shelf, no bespoke wiring, no network expertise, and no programmer on site. If it needs a specialist to make it work, it will not be fitted at volume.
2. **It can be supported at estate scale.** A housing provider responsible for hundreds of homes cannot rely on each resident to configure or diagnose anything. The system has to be commissioned before handover and then managed afterwards by the provider's own staff — remotely, from a phone, across every property they run.

## Where RBR fits

RBR was built to those two bars, and has been in development, on and off, since 2020 — through several revisions. It runs on a small, inexpensive computer in the home, talks to off-the-shelf wireless thermometers and radiator controls, and is managed from a phone — by the resident in a single home, or by the provider across an estate. Because it is software, the parts can be bought anywhere and replaced by the same trade that fitted the rest of the system.

## What we are not claiming yet

Being honest about the limits is part of making the case, so the open questions are stated here rather than buried.

- **Two installations.** RBR runs in two homes today, and no running-cost figure in these documents is a measurement: there is no before-and-after data for either installation, so the saving is argued from behaviour rather than from meter readings. [Costs and payback](05-costs-and-payback.md) sets out what that does and does not allow us to claim.
- **Compliance.** How room-by-room control sits with building regulations, boiler interlocks and heat-pump warranty terms is not written up yet. It is a question a heating engineer will rightly ask first, and it is on the list.
- **Policy.** This document argues a direction, not a scheme. No national programme is assumed, and the case does not depend on one arriving.
