# The experience is the product

> **Audience:** industry — specifiers, heating contractors, developers, housing providers · **Status:** draft · **Last verified:** 2026-09-20 · **See also:** [Why room by room](01-why-room-by-room.md) · [What RBR is](02-what-rbr-is.md) · [Glossary](../appendix/glossary.md)

## You already buy this way

Ask someone why they chose their car and they will not describe the engine. A BMW, a Fiat and a Ford are, mechanically, much the same proposition — wheels, an engine, seats, somewhere to put the shopping — and buyers nonetheless hold intense preferences between them. Step sideways to computers and it happens again: a computer is a processor, memory, a screen, a keyboard and a pointing device, and the choice people actually make is between Windows, Apple and Linux. A phone repeats the pattern, with a little haptics added at the top, and its owners divide into iPhone and Android.

Picture it as a pyramid. Every layer of the hardware is real, and every layer is taken for granted. At the top sits the part people choose on: how the thing feels to own and to use, and who stands behind it. Nobody is unaware of the machinery. It is simply not where the decision is made.

## A heating system is a product too

A room-by-room heating system is not a special case. It is a product: bought, installed, lived with and eventually replaced. And the hardware layer of that product is now settled. The parts a system is built from today — a controller, wireless thermometers, powered valves, a relay or two — are much the same parts, from much the same suppliers, as the commercial systems that arrived a few years ago and never gathered momentum. The hardware was never the obstacle.

Those systems stalled a layer above the hardware. They asked the household to be its own installer, its own network administrator and its own support desk, and most households declined. The evidence is set out in [Why room by room](01-why-room-by-room.md): the demand was there, the appetite was there, and the friction at the top of the pyramid defeated both.

## The awkward part: no brand

The car, computer and phone examples share something RBR does not have: a brand. Someone choosing a BMW is buying an identity as much as a car, and a new entrant cannot borrow that.

So for a system with no badge behind it, the promise has to be made a different way. Two things have to stand in for the brand. The experience itself has to be plainly better where it counts — in the fitting, in the first week, and in the third year when something goes wrong. And there has to be somebody behind it: for a household, the installer or landlord who will answer; for a housing provider, a supplier whose systems are managed across the estate rather than left with each resident.

## What RBR does about it

RBR is aimed deliberately at that top layer. Three choices make it up.

**An interface built for one job.** Heating is a small, specific task with a handful of things a person wants to do: warm this room now, warm it at these times, leave that room alone. RBR's interface is built around those few actions, not around the generalist home-automation platforms that hand a household a box of generic parts and the job of assembling them.

**A system written to be changed.** RBR is written in a small, high-level form that is meant to be edited, not rebuilt, so adapting it to a changed requirement — a different tariff, a new building regulation, a fashion in how people set their heating — is a matter of amending a script rather than commissioning a new product. How that is done belongs to the technical layer; what matters here is that the shape of the thing can follow the shape of the need.

**Built quickly, and fixed quickly.** RBR is developed with AI assistance rather than a conventional programming team. The point for a buyer is not the tool but the consequence: the code stays small, it carries none of the framework dependencies that quietly go stale, and a fix, a feature or a new idea can move from being noticed to being in place far faster, and far more cheaply, than an ordinary product cycle allows. From the outside, that is simply a system that keeps up.

## A common platform, and the competition it invites

RBR is built on standard, openly available parts, and that is a position rather than a procurement convenience. It also settles where the competition belongs. Windows and Linux run on identical hardware and compete entirely on what sits above it, and a heating system should be no different: if room-by-room control becomes normal in new houses, the parts will be much the same in everyone's system, and the argument will be about how each one behaves, how it is fitted, and who supports it.

The same position is what lets RBR accommodate different hardware. Because the system supplies the layer above the parts rather than the parts themselves, it can work with a room wired the conventional way, with a thermometer and a relay driving a valve, or with a self-contained valve that senses and switches on its own — and with whatever replaces both in a few years' time.

That is a market worth having competitors in. RBR does not claim to have the answers for all time, and it would be a poor sign for the category if it did. A common platform that others can build on — differently, or better — is what a healthy market looks like, and it is the outcome this project would rather see than a lock-in of its own.

## The test this sets

If the hardware is not the argument, then the argument has to be won where people live rather than on the specification sheet. That sets the shape of what follows: why the room is the right unit to control, what the product is, and what it is actually like to have one in a house.
